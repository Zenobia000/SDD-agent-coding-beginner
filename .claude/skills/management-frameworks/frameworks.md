# Management Frameworks Knowledge Base

Six categories, matching the decision tree in [SKILL.md](./SKILL.md). Read only the section the diagnosis landed on. Reach scale: Extremely broad > Very broad > Broad > Moderate.

## Goal Setting and Management

| Framework | 中文名稱 | Origin | How to use | Strengths & pitfalls | Notable cases | Reach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **SMART** | SMART原則 | George T. Doran | Check each goal against five criteria: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. | **Strengths:** Clear structure, easy to grasp and apply; improves goal feasibility and success rate; easy to track and evaluate. **Pitfalls:** Can cap creativity and ambition, yielding overly conservative goals; overweights short-term quantitative metrics at the expense of long-term value and qualitative growth. | Nearly universal among companies practicing goal management: GE, Microsoft, and Google require employees to reference SMART when setting individual performance goals. | Extremely broad. From personal life management and project management to corporate performance appraisal — one of the most foundational and widely known goal-setting frameworks. |
| **OKR** | 目標與關鍵結果 | Andy Grove | 1. Set a concise, inspiring, challenging qualitative Objective. 2. Define 3-5 quantifiable, time-bound Key Results per Objective. 3. Track regularly (usually weekly); score 0-1 at cycle end (usually quarterly). | **Strengths:** Drives organizational focus and goal alignment; encourages stretch goals and innovation; high transparency fosters cross-team collaboration. **Pitfalls:** High learning and communication cost early on; tying OKRs directly to performance appraisal makes employees sandbag their KRs; easy to fall into "only doing what gets measured." | Intel: originated and championed by Grove. Google: introduced by John Doerr in 1999; became a core management tool for its rapid growth. | Broad. Popular in tech, startups, and organizations pursuing agile innovation: Spotify, LinkedIn, Netflix. |
| **MBO** | 目標管理 | Peter Drucker | 1. Top management sets overall strategic goals. 2. Managers and reports jointly negotiate, translating organizational goals into measurable department and individual goals. 3. Track regularly with feedback. 4. At cycle end, conduct performance appraisal and rewards based on goal attainment. | **Strengths:** Boosts employee engagement and ownership; ensures individual goals align with the organization's; provides clear performance evaluation standards. **Pitfalls:** Time-consuming and paperwork-heavy; poorly executed, it degrades into top-down commands; may ignore environmental change and lack flexibility. | HP: "The HP Way" deeply integrated the MBO ethos. Widely adopted by traditional manufacturers and large enterprises in the latter half of the 20th century. | Broad. Though challenged by OKR and others, its core ideas still underpin many performance systems. |
| **BHAG** | 宏偉、艱鉅、大膽的目標 | Jim Collins and Jerry Porras | Set a clear, compelling, highly challenging long-term goal, typically requiring 10-30 years, as a "North Star" for direction; must be tightly linked to core values and mission. | **Strengths:** Ignites passion and energy; provides unified long-term direction; promotes breakthrough thinking. **Pitfalls:** High risk — failure can crush morale; too distant to translate easily into short-term action; demands strong leadership and sustained communication to maintain momentum. | Microsoft's early "a computer on every desk and in every home." Boeing's jet-age goal to dominate the jet airliner market, delivering the Boeing 707. | Moderate. Mainly used as a strategic planning tool by ambitious, long-horizon companies; suited to organizations seeking transformation or market leadership. |
| **Goal Pyramid** | 目標金字塔 | No single inventor; draws on multiple psychologists and management scholars. | Decompose top-down from the ultimate goal (life purpose, company vision): top — core values/vision → middle — long-term goals (years) → lower — short-term goals (months/quarters) → base — concrete weekly actions. Ensure base-level actions support the layers above. | **Strengths:** Makes abstract vision concrete; connects daily work to long-term goals; sustains focus. **Pitfalls:** A wrong top-level goal skews the whole system; decomposition can become rigid; requires periodic review or it drifts from reality. | Widely applied in the FranklinCovey time-management system; many productivity tools (e.g., Notion templates) build on this concept. | Broad. Very common in personal development, career planning, and time management; at the organizational level, it is the base logic of strategic planning and goal decomposition. |
| **KPI** | 關鍵績效指標 | Evolved concept, no single inventor; closely related to the Balanced Scorecard. | 1. Clarify strategic goals. 2. Select the quantitative metrics that most directly reflect goal attainment. 3. Set target values. 4. Track regularly via dashboards, analyze and report, and adjust strategy accordingly. | **Strengths:** Objective, quantitative basis for evaluation; quick read on business health; drives performance improvement. **Pitfalls:** "Working for the metric" while ignoring the business meaning behind it; wrong metrics drive wrong behavior; overfocus on lagging indicators at the expense of leading indicators. | Every industry: monthly sales revenue, customer-service satisfaction scores, website conversion rates. KPIs are the core of BI dashboards. | Extremely broad. Nearly all modern companies, regardless of size or industry, use KPIs to manage operational performance. |
| **GROW Model** | GROW模型 | John Whitmore, Alan Fine, Graham Alexander, et al. | One-on-one coaching conversation: G(Goal) — what do you want to achieve? R(Reality) — current situation and resources? O(Options) — what options exist? W(Will) — what will you do, and when will you start? | **Strengths:** Simple structure, easy to learn; grants the coachee autonomy and unlocks potential; effectively clarifies thinking and produces concrete action. **Pitfalls:** Depends on the questioner's skill and neutrality; limited effect on people lacking motivation or domain knowledge; suited to individual development, hard to apply directly to complex organizational goals. | Corporate coaching-style leadership training; the core dialogue framework for professional career/performance coaches. | Broad. A mainstream coaching tool in management, leadership development, HR, education, and personal growth. |
| **Backward Goals** | 逆向目標設定法 | Ancient concept rooted in reverse-engineering thinking; no single inventor. | 1. Vividly envision the final state of the achieved goal. 2. Ask "what must be completed just before that?" 3. Keep deriving backward into milestones from end to start, until you reach today's first action. | **Strengths:** Maps a clear path; surfaces potential obstacles in advance; breaks big goals into small steps, lowering the barrier to action. **Pitfalls:** Unsuitable for exploratory goals with unclear paths; insufficient information can lead to wrong assumptions; requires strong logic and planning skills. | The core spirit of "Begin with the End in Mind" in Covey's *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*; project management routinely back-plans phase timelines from the delivery date. | Broad. Project management, retirement/financial planning, habit building, large-event planning — any domain needing detailed path planning. |
| **HARD Goals** | HARD目標 | Mark Murphy | Set goals that spark intrinsic motivation: H(Heartfelt) — tied to deep values and passion; A(Animated) — vividly visualizable outcome; R(Required) — urgently linked to survival or success; D(Difficult) — challenging enough to unlock potential. | **Strengths:** Emphasizes intrinsic motivation and emotional connection, sustaining lasting passion; compensates for SMART's weakness in motivation and ambition. **Pitfalls:** More subjective, hard to standardize; the "Required" link can be difficult to establish in some contexts. | Case from Murphy's book *HARD Goals*: reframing "grow sales 10%" as "win the market-share battle against our biggest rival and become number one in the region." | Moderate. Gains attention as a SMART complement in leadership training and employee motivation, but less widespread than SMART or OKR. |

## Strategy and Innovative Thinking

| Framework | 中文名稱 | Origin | How to use | Strengths & pitfalls | Notable cases | Reach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **SWOT Analysis** | SWOT分析 | Albert Humphrey | 1. Define the subject (organization/product/individual). 2. Brainstorm internal Strengths (S) and Weaknesses (W), external Opportunities (O) and Threats (T). 3. Cross-match into SO (growth), WO (turnaround), ST (defensive), WT (diversification or retreat) strategies. 4. Build an action plan. | **Strengths:** Simple, intuitive, flexible; quick holistic view of the current situation. **Pitfalls:** Oversimplified; factor definitions are subjective; static analysis lacks timeliness. | Nearly every enterprise: Apple maps brand strength, high-price weakness, emerging-market opportunities, and competitor threats to set market strategy. | Extremely broad. Companies of all sizes, nonprofits, government agencies, even personal career planning. |
| **Porter's Five Forces** | 波特五力分析 | Michael E. Porter | 1. Define industry boundaries. 2. Assess each force: supplier bargaining power, buyer bargaining power, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, intensity of existing rivalry. 3. Judge overall industry competitiveness and profit potential. 4. Craft strategies to improve position, e.g., build barriers to entry. | **Strengths:** Clear structure; systematic analysis of the competitive environment to find profit drivers. **Pitfalls:** Static; ignores macro factors like government and technology; best suited to structurally stable industries. | Nike analyzing the athletic-shoe market: how brand power lowers buyer bargaining power; threats from incumbents like Adidas and emerging brands. | Broad. Competitive strategy, industry-attractiveness assessment, investor industry analysis. |
| **PESTEL Analysis** | PESTEL分析 | Francis Aguilar | 1. Pick the market or country to analyze. 2. Gather macro trends across Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, and Legal dimensions. 3. Identify the highest-impact key drivers. 4. Assess opportunities and threats and fold them into strategic planning. | **Strengths:** Highly comprehensive; captures macro-environment trends for early positioning. **Pitfalls:** Data gathering is time-consuming; factors are interrelated; hard to predict sudden events. | Tesla, before entering new markets, analyzing each country's EV subsidies (political), environmental awareness (social), and charging infrastructure (technological). | Broad. International firms, new-market entry assessment, long-term strategic planning, risk management. |
| **BCG Matrix** | BCG矩陣 (波士頓矩陣) | Bruce Henderson / Boston Consulting Group | 1. Divide the business into strategic business units (SBUs). 2. Plot four quadrants with market growth rate on the vertical axis and relative market share on the horizontal. 3. Classify each SBU as Star, Cash Cow, Question Mark, or Dog. 4. Set invest, hold, harvest, or divest strategies accordingly. | **Strengths:** Easy to grasp; visualizes the product portfolio for executive resource-allocation decisions. **Pitfalls:** Only two dimensions; ignores synergies; market share and profitability are not necessarily correlated. | Google: search is the cash cow supplying stable cash flow; emerging AI or cloud businesses are question marks or stars requiring continued investment. | Broad. Portfolio management and resource allocation in large multi-product-line companies. |
| **Blue Ocean Strategy** | 藍海策略 | W. Chan Kim & Renée Mauborgne | 1. Use a "strategy canvas" to map the industry's competitive factors and rivals' performance. 2. Apply the four-actions framework: Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create. 3. Break the value–cost trade-off to forge a new value curve and market space. | **Strengths:** Encourages fundamental innovation; escapes zero-sum competition; creates high-growth, high-margin new markets. **Pitfalls:** High risk and uncertainty; success invites imitation, quickly turning the blue ocean red. | Cirque du Soleil removed animal acts and expensive star performers, fused theater with art, and drew adult audiences beyond traditional circus-goers. | Moderate. Firms seeking breakthrough growth; requires a strong innovation culture and resources. |
| **Design Thinking** | 設計思考 | Tim Brown & David Kelley / popularized by IDEO | 1. Empathize: understand user needs and pain points. 2. Define: converge on the core problem. 3. Ideate: generate many solutions. 4. Prototype: low-cost interactive models. 5. Test: rapid correction from user feedback. Non-linear iteration. | **Strengths:** Human-centered; solves real user problems; yields innovative, well-received products. **Pitfalls:** Time-consuming; hard to apply to non-concrete systemic problems; outcomes are hard to quantify. | Airbnb's founders personally lived the guest experience, refining listing presentation and booking flow to greatly boost experience and trust. | Extremely broad. Product design, service design, process optimization, business model innovation, social-problem solving. |
| **Lean Startup** | 精實創業 | Eric Ries | 1. From the business model, state key hypotheses about value and growth. 2. Build a minimum viable product (MVP) to test core hypotheses. 3. Enter the Build–Measure–Learn loop, validating with real user data. 4. Based on learnings, decide to pivot or persevere. | **Strengths:** Lowers startup failure risk and cost; accelerates product–market fit; avoids building products nobody wants. **Pitfalls:** Over-focus on iteration can obscure long-term vision; ill-suited to disruptive technologies requiring long R&D. | Dropbox first shot a video demonstrating the core feature (MVP), validating demand for file sync before investing in development. | Broad. Standard method for tech startups; internal innovation units at large firms use it for new products or markets. |
| **Business Model Canvas** | 商業模式圖 | Alexander Osterwalder | 1. Prepare the nine-block canvas. 2. As a team, fill in: customer segments, value proposition, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners, cost structure. 3. Examine the logic linking the nine blocks; assess feasibility and risk. | **Strengths:** Visual and structured; gives the team a shared understanding of the business model, aiding discussion and innovation. **Pitfalls:** Static; omits competitive analysis; can slide into oversimplified thinking. | Uber: connects riders (customers) with drivers (partners), delivers value via the app (channel), and takes a cut (revenue stream). | Extremely broad. From startups to large enterprises for designing, describing, evaluating, and innovating business models; a staple in business schools and entrepreneurship courses. |
| **Ansoff Matrix** | 安索夫矩陣 | Igor Ansoff | 1. Plot four quadrants with product (existing/new) on the horizontal axis and market (existing/new) on the vertical. 2. Position strategies: market penetration, product development, market development, diversification. 3. Risk rises from market penetration to diversification. 4. Choose a growth path by resources and risk tolerance. | **Strengths:** Simple structure; clearly presents four growth strategies. **Pitfalls:** Ignores competitors and market complexity; offers no execution method. | Starbucks: opening more stores is market penetration; bottled coffee in supermarkets is market development; new-flavor food items are product development. | Broad. Any company weighing growth paths; a common tool in strategy-planning sessions. |
| **SCAMPER** | 奔馳法 | Bob Eberle | 1. Pick the product/service/process to improve. 2. Question it through seven lenses: Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify (magnify/minify), Put to another use, Eliminate, Rearrange/Reverse. 3. Filter for feasible ideas. | **Strengths:** A concrete ideation path; simple to run; breaks fixed thinking and sparks many ideas. **Pitfalls:** Suited to improving existing things; limited for from-scratch original invention. | McDonald's drive-thru: Combine restaurant and car; Rearrange the ordering and pickup flow. | Broad. Product development, advertising creative, process improvement, problem solving; works for individuals or teams. |
| **First Principles Thinking** | 第一性原理思考 | Traceable to Aristotle; popularized by Elon Musk | 1. Decompose the problem into the most basic, irreducible facts. 2. Question all existing assumptions, especially reasoning by analogy rather than fact. 3. Rebuild a better solution from fundamentals, unconstrained by existing frameworks. | **Strengths:** Escapes conventional thinking and path dependence; produces disruptive innovation; solves problems at the root. **Pitfalls:** Extremely effortful and time-consuming; demands deep knowledge; no guarantee of a better solution. | Musk founding SpaceX: rejected the conventional view that rockets must be expensive, worked back to raw-material costs, found dramatic reductions possible, and disrupted aerospace. | Moderate. Major technological innovation and hard, complex problems; suits entrepreneurs, scientists, engineers. |

## Problem Analysis and Solving

| Framework | 中文名稱 | Origin | How to use | Strengths & pitfalls | Notable cases | Reach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **5 Whys** | 5問法 | Taiichi Ohno (Toyota) | Ask "why" at least five times in succession until the root cause emerges. Steps: form team → define problem → ask successive whys → identify root cause → devise countermeasures → monitor results. | **Strengths:** Simple, fast, intuitive; pushes teams to think deeper. **Pitfalls:** Multiple root causes may exist; results depend on the questioner's subjectivity; suits only relatively simple causal chains. | Toyota Production System: a robot stopped; successive whys traced it to a missing filter letting metal shavings clog the oil pump. | Broad. Manufacturing quality management, software debugging, project management, everyday problem solving. |
| **Fishbone Diagram** | 魚骨圖 (石川圖) | Kaoru Ishikawa | State the problem at the fish head; branch possible causes (medium bones) from main dimensions (big bones) — Man, Machine, Material, Method, Environment, Measurement — drilling down (small bones) to the most likely cause. | **Strengths:** Structured, visually clear, encourages comprehensive thinking, aids brainstorming. **Pitfalls:** Can get complex; doesn't show relationships between causes; time-consuming. | Mazda used it developing the MX-5 (Miata) to achieve the "rider and horse as one" driving feel. | Broad. Quality improvement, process optimization, and product design in manufacturing, services, healthcare, software engineering. |
| **Root Cause Analysis (RCA)** | 根本原因分析 | No single inventor; an integrative concept | Structured methodology integrating multiple tools (5 Whys, fishbone) to probe a problem's origin. Core principle: don't just treat symptoms — find and fix the system or process flaw that caused the problem. | **Strengths:** Solves the underlying problem, prevents recurrence, fosters a continuous improvement culture. **Pitfalls:** Complex and time-consuming; needs cross-department collaboration; identifying all root causes can be hard. | NASA's Challenger disaster analysis: beyond the technical failure, it dug into organizational culture and decision-making processes. | Broad. High-risk industries (aviation, nuclear, healthcare), plus ITSM, manufacturing, project management. |
| **PDCA Cycle** | PDCA循環 | W. Edwards Deming | Continuous improvement loop: Plan (set goals and plan) → Do → Check (measure gap between results and goals) → Act (adjust, standardize successes, or start a new cycle). | **Strengths:** Simple logic, highly general, emphasizes continuous improvement, encourages data-driven decisions. **Pitfalls:** Looks simple but hard to execute; too slow for urgent problems; requires full-team commitment. | Widely applied in quality management systems (ISO 9001) and continuous improvement processes across industries worldwide. | Very broad. Nearly all management activity — product development, project management, personal goals. |
| **8D Problem Solving** | 8D問題解決法 | Ford Motor Company | Eight disciplines: D1 form team, D2 describe problem, D3 interim containment, D4 define and verify root cause, D5 select and verify permanent countermeasure, D6 implement permanent countermeasure, D7 prevent recurrence, D8 recognize the team. | **Strengths:** Rigorous, complete process; emphasizes teamwork; establishes a standardized problem-solving workflow. **Pitfalls:** Cumbersome, time-consuming, not suited to all problem types. | Standard process in automotive and electronics manufacturing for resolving supplier and customer quality complaints. | Moderate. Mainly manufacturing: quality and supply chain management in automotive, aerospace, electronics. |
| **MECE Principle** | MECE原則 | Barbara Minto (developed at McKinsey) | Decompose a problem into components that are mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. Often paired with an issue tree to ensure no overlap and no gaps. | **Strengths:** Clear structure, prevents omissions, improves communication, simplifies complex problems. **Pitfalls:** Highly complex or dynamic problems resist full exhaustiveness; can oversimplify. | Core thinking discipline at McKinsey, BCG, Bain for market analysis and profit-improvement engagements. | Broad. Structured-thinking domains: management consulting, corporate strategy, market analysis, product management. |
| **Issue Tree** | 議題樹 / 邏輯樹 | Originated at consulting firms such as McKinsey | Decompose the core problem or hypothesis (trunk) layer by layer into smaller sub-problems (branches), each layer MECE. Two types: problem tree (Why Tree) and solution tree (How Tree). | **Strengths:** Breaks down big problems, visualizes thinking, ensures systematic completeness. **Pitfalls:** Time-consuming to build; hard to decompose effectively without domain familiarity. | Consultants on "how to increase company profit": split into revenue and cost, then subdivide to locate the problem. | Broad. Problem decomposition in management consulting, business analysis, strategic planning, academic research. |
| **Six Thinking Hats** | 六頂思考帽 | Edward de Bono | Role-play six colored hats to direct thinking: white = information, red = emotion, black = risk, yellow = benefits, green = creativity, blue = process control. The whole team wears the same hat at the same time. | **Strengths:** Parallel thinking avoids argument, enables comprehensive objective analysis, improves meeting efficiency, sparks creativity. **Pitfalls:** Requires a skilled facilitator (blue hat); overkill for simple problems. | Corporate brainstorming, strategy meetings, product evaluation: assessing a launch plan by wearing each hat in turn. | Broad. Business meetings, education, ideation, decision making, conflict management. |
| **DMAIC** | DMAIC模型 | Part of Six Sigma, popularized by Motorola | Six Sigma's core process for improving existing processes: Define → Measure → Analyze → Improve → Control. | **Strengths:** Data-driven, rigorous process, focused on reducing variation and defects, measurable results. **Pitfalls:** Complex; requires professional training (Green/Black Belts); can stifle incremental innovation; high implementation cost. | GE rolled it out company-wide under Jack Welch, saving billions of dollars. | Broad. Industries pursuing highly stable processes and consistent quality: manufacturing, finance, healthcare. |
| **TRIZ** | 萃智理論 | Genrich Altshuller | Derived from analyzing vast numbers of patents worldwide: 40 inventive principles plus a contradiction matrix. Solves technical contradictions and engineering problems systematically rather than by trial and error or inspiration. | **Strengths:** Systematic innovation method; breaks mental fixation; resolves seemingly unsolvable technical contradictions. **Pitfalls:** Steep learning curve; mainly for technical/engineering problems; abstract terminology. | Samsung embedded it widely in R&D, breaking semiconductor and display technology bottlenecks. | Moderate. Technology-intensive innovation fields: engineering, R&D, product design, patent strategy. |
| **CATWOE Analysis** | CATWOE分析 | Peter Checkland | Part of Soft Systems Methodology (SSM); examines a problem situation from six angles: Customers, Actors, Transformation process, Worldview, Owners, Environmental constraints. | **Strengths:** Surfaces stakeholders' differing perspectives; suits ill-defined "soft" problems; builds a holistic picture of the problem. **Pitfalls:** Subjective, no definitive answer, emphasizes problem definition over concrete solutions. | Public-sector policy planning: "how to improve the NHS" analyzed through the views and expectations of patients, doctors, government, taxpayers. | Moderate. Complex multi-stakeholder problems: organizational change, systems analysis, public policy, process redesign. |

## Process Improvement and Efficiency

| Framework | 中文名稱 | Origin | How to use | Strengths & pitfalls | Notable cases | Reach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Lean Manufacturing / Lean Thinking** | 精實生產 / 精實思維 | Developed post-WWII at Toyota as the Toyota Production System (TPS) by Taiichi Ohno, Shigeo Shingo, and others | Analyze processes with Value Stream Mapping (VSM); identify and eliminate the seven wastes (overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, motion, defects); pursue Just-In-Time (JIT) and Jidoka (autonomation); use continuous improvement to achieve high efficiency, high quality, low cost. | **Strengths:** Sharply cuts waste and cost; raises efficiency, quality, and customer satisfaction; gives employees ownership. **Pitfalls:** Demands a highly stable supply chain; initial cultural resistance; high employee skill requirements; over-pursuing zero inventory raises supply-disruption risk. | Toyota: foundation of its entire production system, outperforming US/European rivals with fewer resources. Widely applied in GE manufacturing. Nike reworked its supply chain, shortening delivery times and reducing waste. | Extremely broad. From manufacturing into services, healthcare, software development (Agile), and government. |
| **Six Sigma** | 六標準差 | Proposed in 1986 by Bill Smith (Motorola engineer); heavily promoted by Jack Welch at GE | Apply DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control) or DMADV (Define, Measure, Analyze, Design, Verify); use rigorous statistical analysis to find and eliminate root causes of process defects and reduce process variability. | **Strengths:** Data-driven; maximizes quality and reliability; lowers defect rates and saves cost; measurable results. **Pitfalls:** Can become rigid and bureaucratic; requires heavy statistical expertise and training (Black/Green Belts); may over-focus on improving existing processes at the expense of innovation. | Motorola saved over $16B in 1987–1997. GE made it a core strategy, yielding billions in benefits. Bank of America optimized customer-service processes to cut error rates. | Broad. From manufacturing quality control into finance, healthcare, IT, and supply chain; especially suited to industries with extreme quality and precision requirements. |
| **Kaizen** | 改善 | Popularized in the West by Masaaki Imai's book *Kaizen*; rooted in the Toyota Production System and Deming's theories | Encourage employees at all levels (executives to front line) to proactively and continuously propose and implement small, ongoing improvements to workflows, environments, and tools. Emphasizes the PDCA cycle. | **Strengths:** Full participation boosts morale and belonging; low cost, low risk; small changes compound into major transformation; builds a continuous improvement culture. **Pitfalls:** Incremental change cannot handle drastic market shifts or breakthrough-innovation needs; requires management support and sustained culture-building. | Toyota: core of company culture; employees average dozens of improvement proposals per year. Sony factory improvement activities. Nestlé's global-plant continuous improvement programs. | Extremely broad. Organizations of any type and size: factories, office administration, software teams, personal life. |
| **TQM** | 全面品質管理 | Evolved from the ideas of quality gurus W. Edwards Deming, Joseph M. Juran, and Philip B. Crosby | Center on the customer; extend quality responsibility to every member and stage. Through full participation, continuous improvement, process orientation, and data-driven decisions, ensure the entire value chain — from product design to after-sales service — meets or exceeds customer expectations. | **Strengths:** Raises customer satisfaction and loyalty; strengthens teamwork and engagement; improves company culture; lowers cost long-term. **Pitfalls:** Long adoption time, slow results; requires sustained top-management commitment and culture change; poorly executed, it degrades into formality or slogans. | Adopted by the US Navy in the 1980s. Ford introduced it during its quality crisis ("Quality is Job 1"). Xerox adopted it early and won the US National Quality Award. | Broad. Peaked in the 1980s–90s; parts absorbed into Lean and Six Sigma; "customer-centricity" and "full participation" are now embedded in modern quality management. |
| **BPM** | 企業流程管理 | A systematic management methodology evolved from TQM and Six Sigma, combined with advances in information technology | Use BPM software or platforms for process Modeling, Execution, Monitoring, and Optimization. Treat business processes as corporate assets to be systematically automated, managed, and continuously improved. | **Strengths:** Improves process transparency and control; automation raises efficiency and consistency; increases agility; eases compliance management. **Pitfalls:** High adoption cost (especially software); requires cross-department coordination; over-focus on process can reduce flexibility. | IBM manages global business processes internally. Siemens standardized global procurement and supply-chain processes. Coca-Cola manages new-product development and launch processes. | Very broad. Industries with complex processes, cross-department collaboration, and heavy regulation: finance, insurance, telecom, manufacturing, public sector. |
| **Kanban (system)** | 看板系統 | Designed by Taiichi Ohno for Toyota in the 1940s as part of the JIT production system | Manage workflow via a visual board (physical or digital); work items move as cards from To Do through In Progress to Done. Core principles: visualize the workflow and limit work in progress (WIP) to keep teams from overloading and work flowing smoothly. | **Strengths:** Simple, intuitive, easy to adopt; improves transparency and surfaces bottlenecks early; reduces work in progress (WIP) and shortens delivery cycles; layers onto existing processes. **Pitfalls:** Ill-suited to complex processes without clear discrete steps; without WIP limits it degenerates into a chaotic to-do list. | Toyota controls parts inventory and production. Microsoft software-development and IT-operations teams. Spotify combines Kanban with Agile methods. | Extremely broad. Manufacturing → software development (popularized by David J. Anderson) → knowledge work: IT, operations, marketing, HR. |
| **5S Methodology** | 5S管理法 | Originated in Japan as part of TPS; specific creator hard to attribute | Five steps in order: Seiri (Sort) — separate necessary from unnecessary and discard; Seiton (Set in order) — assign fixed locations; Seiso (Shine) — clean and remove dirt; Seiketsu (Standardize) — institutionalize the first 3S; Shitsuke (Sustain) — build disciplined habits. | **Strengths:** Clean, safe, orderly environment; raises efficiency and quality; reduces equipment failures; builds good habits. **Pitfalls:** Easily dismissed as mere housecleaning, missing the discipline and cultural spirit; requires ongoing supervision to sustain. | Boeing assembly lines improved efficiency and safety. Intel wafer fabs maintain cleanliness and order. Japanese manufacturers such as Panasonic and Honda. | Extremely broad. Factories, offices, hospitals, labs, even personal home organization (danshari was influenced by it). |
| **Theory of Constraints (TOC)** | 限制理論 | Eliyahu M. Goldratt (Israeli physicist and management consultant), first presented in the 1984 business novel *The Goal* | Manage the bottleneck via Five Focusing Steps: Identify → Exploit the bottleneck's capacity → Subordinate non-bottleneck resources to the bottleneck's pace → Elevate (invest in bottleneck capacity) → Repeat by finding the next bottleneck for continuous improvement. | **Strengths:** Focuses on the critical few; rapidly raises overall throughput; a clear improvement focus avoids waste; high ROI. **Pitfalls:** Over-focusing on a single bottleneck may let secondary problems accumulate; bottlenecks are harder to identify outside production environments. | Ford's paint-shop bottleneck fix sharply raised output. US Air Force optimized aircraft maintenance processes. Delta Air Lines improved operational scheduling and cut delays. | Broad. Production scheduling → project management (CCPM), supply chain (Drum-Buffer-Rope), marketing, sales, strategic planning. |
| **Value Stream Mapping (VSM)** | 價值流程圖 | Rooted in TPS; systematized and popularized by Mike Rother and John Shook in *Learning to See* | Draw a detailed map covering both material and information flows; distinguish value-adding from non-value-adding activities (waste); analyze the end-to-end process from customer order to product delivery; design a leaner "future-state map". | **Strengths:** Macro view of the entire value chain; effectively identifies sources of waste; an essential first step in Lean transformation. **Pitfalls:** Mapping is time-consuming and needs cross-department input; nonlinear or highly customized processes are hard to map; it is only an analysis tool and offers no solutions. | The foundational adoption tool for nearly all Lean enterprises. Starbucks analyzed its coffee-making process, cutting movement and waiting to speed up drink delivery. Healthcare providers map the patient journey from check-in to discharge. | Broad. Any end-to-end process optimization: manufacturing, software, healthcare, finance, government. |

## Communication, Expression, and Decision-Making

| **The Pyramid Principle** | 金字塔原理 | Barbara Minto, former McKinsey consultant | State the central idea or conclusion first (apex), then break it into 3–5 supporting argument groups, each backed by finer data or facts — forming a layered, logically rigorous pyramid. | **Strengths:** Clear structure, easy to grasp; captures attention fast; ideal for time-pressed senior executives. **Pitfalls:** Can oversimplify complex problems; demands rigorous logic training; unsuited to exploratory or brainstorming communication. | Standard communication method at McKinsey, BCG, and Bain for client reports, proposals, and internal communication; a must-have consultant skill. | Extremely broad. Business presentations, research reports, email, management communication, legal argumentation, and other structured expression. |
| **RACI Matrix** | RACI責任分配矩陣 | Origin unclear; evolved gradually within project management and management science | 1. List all tasks or process steps. 2. List all members or roles. 3. Build a matrix assigning each task R (Responsible) / A (Accountable) / C (Consulted) / I (Informed), ensuring each task has exactly one A. | **Strengths:** Clarifies roles and responsibilities; reduces overlap or gaps; improves execution efficiency and accountability. **Pitfalls:** Matrices for large projects become unwieldy; easily turns into a formality that neglects actual communication and collaboration. | Project management in global enterprises: IT system rollouts, new product development, process reengineering — clarifying cross-department responsibilities. | Broad. Project management, process improvement, organizational design, service management (ITIL). |
| **Eisenhower Matrix** | 艾森豪矩陣 | Concept attributed to President Eisenhower; popularized by Stephen Covey | Sort to-dos into four quadrants: important and urgent → handle immediately (Do); important, not urgent → schedule time (Schedule); not important but urgent → delegate (Delegate); neither → eliminate (Delete). | **Strengths:** Simple and intuitive; effectively distinguishes priorities; focuses on high-value activities. **Pitfalls:** Judgments of importance and urgency are subjective; ignores the time or resources tasks require. | Heavily promoted in Covey's *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*; became a globally famous time-management tool. | Very broad. Personal time management, team task allocation, leadership development, daily planning. |
| **Cost-Benefit Analysis** | 成本效益分析 | Traceable to 19th-century French engineer Jules Dupuit; widely used in 20th-century public policy | 1. Identify and quantify all costs (direct, indirect, tangible, intangible). 2. Identify and quantify all benefits. 3. Convert to monetary value and compare — via net present value (NPV) or benefit-cost ratio (BCR) — to judge feasibility. | **Strengths:** Data-driven, objective basis for decisions; assesses investment soundness. **Pitfalls:** Intangible costs and benefits (brand reputation, employee morale) are hard to quantify precisely; future forecasts carry uncertainty. | Mandatory for governments evaluating major public works (high-speed rail, airports, dams); used by enterprises before major capital expenditures and new project investments. | Extremely broad. Government, nonprofit, and business: public policy, project management, financial decisions, environmental assessment, IT investment. |
| **Decision Matrix Analysis** | 決策矩陣分析 | Generic technique for systematic decision-making; a famous variant is Stuart Pugh's 1980s Pugh Matrix | 1. List all viable options. 2. Define evaluation criteria. 3. Set weights for each criterion. 4. Score each option. 5. Multiply scores by weights and sum; the highest weighted total wins. | **Strengths:** Objective, transparent, systematic; suits complex multi-criteria decisions; helps teams reach consensus. **Pitfalls:** Results depend heavily on the choice of criteria and weights, so subjectivity remains; time-consuming. | Engineering: selecting the best design or material. Business: supplier selection, product feature ranking, software procurement evaluation. | Broad. Engineering design, product management, operations, strategic planning — even major personal decisions (choosing a job, buying a home). |
| **RAPID Framework** | RAPID決策模型 | Developed and promoted by Bain & Company | Explicitly assign five roles: Recommend (proposes options), Agree (holds veto power), Perform (executes), Input (provides information and advice), Decide (makes the final call). | **Strengths:** Greatly improves decision speed and quality; clarifies who is accountable; reduces ambiguity and deadlock. **Pitfalls:** Can add bureaucracy; unclear role definitions or weak execution slow things down instead; not suited to every decision type. | Ford used RAPID to overhaul a chaotic decision process and speed up product development; Intel improved global operational decision efficiency. | Moderate to broad. Key decision processes in large or matrix organizations with unclear accountability and slow decisions. |
| **SCQA Framework** | SCQA溝通框架 | Proposed by Barbara Minto in *The Pyramid Principle* as the lead-in to the pyramid structure | Open a communication in sequence: S (Situation) — uncontested background → C (Complication) — conflict or change → Q (Question) — the core question → A (Answer) — state the core answer directly. | **Strengths:** Builds consensus quickly, captures interest, cleanly surfaces the core issue and claim. **Pitfalls:** Fixed format; used poorly it feels contrived and formulaic; not right for every opening. | The classic opening structure management consultants use for client proposals and briefings, quickly pulling clients into the thinking frame. | Broad. Consulting, sales, marketing, ad copy, and the argumentative openings of academic writing. |
| **Johari Window** | 周哈里窗 | Proposed by psychologists Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham in 1955 | Divide self-awareness into four areas: Open, Blind, Hidden, Unknown. Expand the Open area through self-disclosure and shrink the Blind area by requesting feedback, improving self-knowledge and relationships. | **Strengths:** Raises self-awareness; fosters trust and open communication; improves team dynamics. **Pitfalls:** Requires a safe, trusting environment; poorly delivered feedback can cause harm; may touch sensitive personal issues. | Team-building activities, leadership workshops, and counseling and coaching programs at companies worldwide. | Broad. HR, organizational development, psychology, team management, personal growth and coaching. |
| **Transactional Analysis (TA)** | 溝通分析理論 | Founded by psychiatrist Eric Berne in the 1950s | Analyze which of three ego states — Parent (P), Adult (A), Child (C) — each "transaction" in an interaction occurs between. Psychotherapy, counseling, education, organizational behavior, management, sales, and customer service. |

## Project and Team Management

| Framework | 中文名稱 | Origin | How to use | Strengths & pitfalls | Notable cases | Reach |

| **Waterfall Model** | 瀑布模型 | First formally described in Winston W. Royce's 1970 paper; the name was coined later by others | Fixed linear phases: requirements analysis → system design → implementation → testing → delivery → maintenance. Each phase must be completed before the next begins. | **Strengths:** Clear structure, easy to manage; well-defined phase goals; suits projects with stable requirements. **Pitfalls:** Inflexible, hard to accommodate requirement changes; late testing means problems surface late and cost more to fix. | Early large government projects (defense, NASA), construction, manufacturing, and traditional software systems with well-defined requirements (banking, healthcare). | Broad. Physical industries such as construction, manufacturing, and engineering, plus traditional IT projects with stable requirements and thorough upfront planning. |
| **Agile Methodology** | 敏捷開發 | The Manifesto for Agile Software Development, published in 2001 by 17 software developers (including Jeff Sutherland and Ken Schwaber); a set of values and principles | Short iteration cycles, each delivering a working software increment. Continuous feedback, team collaboration, rapid response — embrace change. Scrum and Kanban are concrete implementation frameworks. | **Strengths:** Highly adaptive, responds quickly to change; improves customer satisfaction; fosters team communication and collaboration. **Pitfalls:** Final scope and schedule are uncertain; requires heavy customer involvement; hard to adopt in large or bureaucratic organizations. | Cornerstone of modern tech and software: product development at Google, Spotify, Netflix, Microsoft. | Broad. Software development, product management, marketing, R&D — complex projects with high uncertainty and fast-changing requirements. |
| **Scrum** | Scrum框架 | Created by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland in the early 1990s; formally presented in 1995 | Fixed sprint cycles (typically 2–4 weeks) to complete selected work. Three roles (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Development Team), three artifacts (product backlog, sprint backlog, deliverable increment), five events (sprint, sprint planning, daily standup, sprint review, sprint retrospective). | **Strengths:** Clear role accountability; high transparency exposes problems fast; fixed cadence aids focus. **Pitfalls:** An undisciplined Product Owner invites scope creep; poor execution degenerates into "Scrum-but" pseudo-agile. | Dominates software development: most teams at Google and Amazon. Non-software firms like 3M use it for new product development. | Broad. Primarily software and product development; gradually expanding into marketing, HR, operations, and other non-IT domains. |
| **Kanban (method)** | 看板方法 | Original concept created by Taiichi Ohno for Toyota in the 1940s; applied to knowledge work and software development by David J. Anderson in the early 2000s | Visualize the workflow on a kanban board; task cards move left to right. Core practices: visualize the workflow, limit work in progress (WIP), manage flow, make process policies explicit, improve continuously. | **Strengths:** Highly flexible with no fixed iteration cycle; focuses on continuous delivery and flow efficiency; easy to introduce into existing processes. **Pitfalls:** No timebox pressure can mean lack of urgency; demands strong team self-discipline. | Toyota (manufacturing), Pixar (animation production pipeline), Microsoft (IT operations and software development). | Broad. IT operations, DevOps, customer support, recruiting pipelines — work settings emphasizing continuous flow and delivery. |
| **PMBOK** | 專案管理知識體系 | Project Management Institute (PMI); a body of knowledge compiling global best practices, not an individual invention | A "guide," not a methodology: a framework of five process groups and ten knowledge areas; project managers select suitable tools and techniques per project. Basis of the PMP certification. | **Strengths:** Globally shared standardized vocabulary and framework; comprehensive, authoritative body of knowledge. **Pitfalls:** Rigidly applying every process becomes bureaucratic; traditionally suited waterfall (newer editions incorporate substantial agile content). | Millions of PMP-certified professionals worldwide: IBM (IT), Boeing (aerospace), Bechtel (construction). | Very broad. Projects in virtually every industry; the common language and foundational knowledge base of project management. |
| **PRINCE2** | 受控環境下的專案管理 | Developed by the UK government's CCTA in 1989; now owned by AXELOS | Process-driven: seven principles, seven themes, seven processes. IT, public sector, construction, finance — projects needing tight control and governance; strongest in the UK, Europe, and Australia. |
| **WBS** | 工作分解結構 | Concept originated in the US Department of Defense's 1957 PERT program (Polaris missile project), later adopted and promoted by NASA | Decompose the final deliverable, deliverable-oriented, layer by layer into smaller, manageable work packages. Universal foundational project-management tool; indispensable in construction, aerospace, defense, engineering, and large IT projects. |
| **Critical Path Method (CPM)** | 要徑法 | Developed in the late 1950s by Morgan R. Walker of DuPont and James E. Kelley Jr. of Remington Rand, for chemical plant maintenance projects | 1. Find the longest continuous path — the critical path; any delay on it directly delays completion. | **Strengths:** Identifies the most critical tasks; precisely predicts the shortest project duration; optimizes scheduling and resource allocation. **Pitfalls:** Ignores resource constraints (unlike CCPM); duration estimates may be inaccurate; large network diagrams get very complex. | First used for DuPont plant construction and maintenance. Now the standard scheduling and control technique in construction, manufacturing, and engineering. | Broad. Projects with relatively fixed task sequences and durations: construction, manufacturing, large hardware installation. |
| **CCPM** | 關鍵鏈專案管理 | Proposed by Theory of Constraints (TOC) master Eliyahu M. Goldratt in his 1997 book *Critical Chain* | 1. Management shifts to monitoring buffer consumption rate rather than individual task deadlines. | **Strengths:** Handles uncertainty effectively; usually shortens project duration; focuses on finishing fast rather than hitting dates. **Pitfalls:** Requires a major mindset shift; complex to adopt and compute; aggressive duration estimates create pressure. | US Air Force, Boeing, and Lucent Technologies used it to dramatically shorten delivery of complex projects. | Moderate. Shared-resource, highly complex environments: new product development, aerospace engineering, large software R&D. |
| **Extreme Programming (XP)** | 極限編程 | Created by Kent Beck in the late 1990s on the Chrysler C3 payroll project | An agile method emphasizing technical excellence; 12 core practices: pair programming, test-driven development (TDD), continuous integration, small releases, simple design, collective code ownership, etc. | **Strengths:** High-quality, low-defect software; frequent delivery and feedback yield high customer satisfaction; improves team communication. **Pitfalls:** Demands strong discipline, ideally co-located; heavy customer involvement required; pair programming is seen as doubling labor cost. | Chrysler C3 project. Universally applicable to any team: corporate departments, project groups, sports teams, academic groups. |
